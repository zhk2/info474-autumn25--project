The Trade War

How Tariffs and Global Trade Affect the World at Large

Since World War II, international trade has become the backbone of the global economy. Roughly 60% of world GDP now crosses borders, connecting billions of workers, consumers, and businesses in an intricate web of exchange. This integration has generated unprecedented prosperity, lifting over a billion people out of poverty and enabling specialization that makes everything from smartphones to automobiles affordable. But it has also created new vulnerabilities. When trade flows are disrupted by tariffs, pandemics, or geopolitical conflict, the consequences ripple across continents within days.

Our goal is to explore what international trade looks like today, and make sense of the causes — and effects — of tariffs.

How Different Countries Trade

Today, international trade forms an intricate web connecting nearly every nation on Earth. Unlike the relatively isolated economies of the 1930s, modern supply chains span continents—a smartphone assembled in China contains components from Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and rare earth elements from multiple African nations, all designed in California. This interconnectedness means that trade policy decisions in one country ripple across the global economy almost instantly. When the United States imposes tariffs on steel, it affects not just steel producers in China or the European Union, but also car manufacturers in Germany, construction companies in Brazil, and appliance makers in South Korea who all depend on competitively priced materials.

Global trade today accounts for roughly 60% of world GDP, compared to just 9% in 1913. This integration has lifted over a billion people out of poverty, particularly in Asia, by enabling specialization and efficiency at an unprecedented scale. But this same interconnectedness creates vulnerability: disruptions to trade no longer stay contained. A port closure in Shanghai affects inventory in Stuttgart; a semiconductor shortage in Taiwan halts automobile production in Detroit. Understanding trade flows between nations is essential to comprehending how economic decisions affect employment, prices, and prosperity across the world.

The Key Players

The concentration of global trade power is striking. The United States and China alone account for over $7 trillion in annual imports, roughly double the combined total of Germany, the Netherlands, Japan, the United Kingdom, and France. These economic giants don't just buy goods for their own consumption. They serve as critical nodes in global supply chains, importing raw materials and intermediate products that get transformed and re-exported around the world.

This concentration creates both efficiency and fragility. When these major importers impose tariffs or face economic disruption, the effects cascade through dozens of smaller trading partners.

The Impact of Tariffs

On June 17th, 1930, President Herbert Hoover signed a piece of legislation that over one thousand economists had begged him not to sign. Within two years, global trade had collapsed by 66 percent. Within three years, unemployment in America had gone from 8 to 25 percent. And it started because a senator from Utah really wanted to protect sugar beets.

Reed Smoot had been a senator from Utah for 27 years. His confirmation hearings alone had taken four years because of controversy over whether a Mormon church leader should hold federal office, but despite navigating decades of religious controversy, Smoot would become most famous for co-sponsoring the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act.

The tariff started small; Smoot wanted to protect Utah's sugar beet farmers. Then every other congressman wanted to protect their district too. By the time it passed, over 20,000 goods had new tariffs. The average tariff rate jumped from 45% to nearly 60%. Within three years, U.S. imports fell 61%. Exports fell 67%. And while the Great Depression was already underway, economists widely agree that Smoot-Hawley made it catastrophically worse. By 1934, the Reciprocal Tariff Act had reversed many of the tariffs imposed by the Smoot-Hawley Act.

Since the Great Depression, international trade has increased significantly, and plays a large part in our global economy and the effects it has on the people living in it.

Before diving into more details about tariffs, its important to understand some terminology related to trade.

  • Imports: Goods brought into a country from another country
  • Exports: Goods sold to a country from another country
  • Tariff: A tax on imports and exports
  • Trade Balance: Difference between a country's total imports and exports
  • Year-over-Year Trade Balance: Difference in value of a country's imports and exports in one year

The theory behind tariffs is straightforward: raise the price of foreign goods, and domestic industries become more competitive. When the United States imposed tariffs on Canadian goods beginning in 2024, the effect was visible, and reciporical Tariffs gave way to a ~2T decrease in US Exports to Canada.

This only tells part of the story. In many cases, Chinese manufacturers simply rerouted products through third countries or relocated final assembly operations to avoid tariffs. The goods still arrived in American ports, just with different labels. Meanwhile, China's export sector proved more resilient than expected, finding new markets and moving up the value chain into higher-end manufacturing that faced less tariff pressure.

The earlier and later periods cluster closer together with lower volatility, even though tariffs never fully return to their old baseline.

This pattern suggests that trade policy shocks leave a clear imprint but do not lock the system into permanent crisis. As you reach the end of the story, the open question is what the next shock will look like and which countries and sectors will be ready for it when it comes.
