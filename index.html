The Trade War

How Tariffs and Global Trade Affect the World at Large

On June 17th, 1930, President Herbert Hoover signed a piece of legislation that over one thousand economists had begged him not to sign. Within two years, global trade had collapsed by 66 percent. Within three years, unemployment in America had gone from 8 to 25 percent. And it started because a senator from Utah really wanted to protect sugar beets.

Reed Smoot had been a senator from Utah for 27 years. His confirmation hearings alone had taken four years because of controversy over whether a Mormon church leader should hold federal office, but despite navigating decades of religious controversy, Smoot would become most famous for co-sponsoring the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act.

The tariff started small; Smoot wanted to protect Utah's sugar beet farmers. Then every other congressman wanted to protect their district too. By the time it passed, over 20,000 goods had new tariffs. The average tariff rate jumped from 45% to nearly 60%. Within three years, U.S. imports fell 61%. Exports fell 67%. And while the Great Depression was already underway, economists widely agree that Smoot-Hawley made it catastrophically worse. By 1934, the Reciprocal Tariff Act had reversed many of the tariffs imposed by the Smoot-Hawley Act.

Since the Great Depression, international trade has increased significantly, and plays a large part in our global economy and the effects it has on the people living in it.

Our goal is to explore what international trade looks like today, and make sense of the causes — and effects — of tariffs.

How Different Countries Trade

International trade is the system by which countries exchange goods and services across borders. Nations export what they produce efficiently and import what they need, creating a complex web of economic relationships that spans the entire globe.

The Impact of Tariffs

Looking across partners, a single rule does not explain every line in the chart. Higher tariffs often line up with softer import growth, but there are clear exceptions. Some countries appear to re-route supply chains or shift what they sell rather than simply losing access to the United States market.

These patterns remind us that tariffs are part of a longer negotiation. Firms can change suppliers, governments can sign new agreements, and demand can move to different goods. The visualizations on the right capture that tug-of-war in the data rather than a neat “before and after.”

Looking into the Future

The final view pulls back from individual countries and goods to look at the rhythm of the past decade. We compare three periods in the recent trade story: the years before the conflict intensified, the peak of tariff battles, and the more recent period where tensions cooled but never fully disappeared.

Each point combines how aggressive tariff changes were with how volatile global trade became from year to year. The 2018–2020 period stands out with both higher tariff tension and larger swings in trade. The earlier and later periods cluster closer together with lower volatility, even though tariffs never fully return to their old baseline.

This pattern suggests that trade policy shocks leave a clear imprint but do not lock the system into permanent crisis. As you reach the end of the story, the open question is what the next shock will look like and which countries and sectors will be ready for it when it comes.
