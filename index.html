The Trade War

How Tariffs and Global Trade Affect the World at Large

On June 17th, 1930, President Herbert Hoover signed a piece of legislation that over one thousand economists had begged him not to sign. Within two years, global trade had collapsed by 66 percent. Within three years, unemployment in America had gone from 8 to 25 percent. And it started because a senator from Utah really wanted to protect sugar beets.

Reed Smoot had been a senator from Utah for 27 years. He was also a high-ranking apostle in the Mormon Church, one of the first to serve in the Senate. His confirmation hearings alone had taken four years because of controversy over whether a church leader should hold federal office, but despite navigating decades of religious controversy, Smoot would become most famous for co-sponsoring the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act.

The tariff started small; Smoot wanted to protect Utah's sugar beet farmers. Then every other congressman wanted to protect their district too. By the time it passed, over 20,000 goods had new tariffs. The average tariff rate jumped from 45% to nearly 60%. Within three years, U.S. imports fell 61%. Exports fell 67%. And while the Great Depression was already underway, economists widely agree that Smoot-Hawley made it catastrophically worse.

Our goal is to explore what international trade looks like today, and make sense of the causes - and effects - of tariffs.
